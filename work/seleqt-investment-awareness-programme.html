Case study · SELEQT Wealth

Getting doctors to sit down for 45 minutes about money.

SELEQT Wealth runs an Investment Awareness Programme: a free session at a hospital, for doctors, with nothing sold in the room. Our job was every word that had to get them there.

The brief

Sell nothing, in writing.

A wealth firm writing to doctors has one obvious problem: everybody writes to doctors. Pharma, insurance, equipment, apps, and a steady drip of “Sir, just one amazing investment opportunity.” Anything that smells like a pitch is deleted by reflex.

So the rule for every piece was the same, and it was a hard one to write inside: the programme sells nothing. No product names. No individual advice. No collecting numbers in the room. If the writing could not be interesting without a hook at the end, the writing was not good enough.

The second problem was tone. Financial content about doctors usually arrives as a warning: you are behind, you are exposed, act now. Fear works once and then it is the reason nobody replies to the second mail.

What we wrote

Nineteen pieces, one voice.

all of it is on the work page, in full.

  • Twelve outreach mails to hospitals in Ahmedabad and Surat, each with its own way in.
  • A LinkedIn message from the founder's account, opening with “this is just a 9-second read”.
  • The business page copy: private wealth for Indian, NRI and globally connected families, in two versions.

Read them all on the work page: the posts, the mails and the short pieces.

The thinking

Three decisions that shaped everything.

1. Make the reader the expert, not the patient

The posts never explain money to a doctor as though they were slow. They borrow the reader's own professional logic and turn it around: prevention over cure, don't ignore symptoms, small problems stay small when you notice them early. One post is built entirely on that mirror, because a doctor arguing with that argument would be arguing with themselves.

2. Teach one thing completely, and give it away

The best-performing shape was the piece that answers a question fully, with nothing held back for a call. The nominee post is the clearest example: it explains that a nominee is not an heir, names the 2015 insurance exception, and gives a three-step afternoon's fix, ending with a question nobody can answer confidently: do you know who is nominated on your PF account?

3. One programme, twelve doors

The mails share a body because the offer does not change. What changes is the way in. A hospital run by a trust got “Jay Swaminarayan” and “we don't want anything in return, except some blessings.” A hospital with a well-known name in the subject got the name. A heart institute got “Prevention of Financial Attack on doctors.” Several mails carried three subject lines so the client could pick the risk level they were comfortable with.

Same offer. Different door. That is most of what outreach writing is.

What we would not do

No fake urgency, no invented statistics, no “last two seats”. One number appears across the whole programme, on inflation, and it is stated as an example rather than a scare. For a financial firm talking to a regulated profession, the restraint is the product.
