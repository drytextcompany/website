Your away message is the only staff on duty at 11 at night.

Someone asks about a root canal after dinner. What they get back was written once, on the day the app was installed, and it says their message is important to you. Here is how to write it so it helps.

Granth Hirapara2 November 20266 minute read

nobody has ever felt important because a machine said so.

The WhatsApp Business app gives you three small pieces of writing: the away message, the greeting and the quick replies. They sit in the business tools in its settings. Most owners fill them in once, in a hurry, and never read them again. Between them they are probably the most sent words in the business.

The usual text is polite and empty. “Thank you for contacting us. We will revert at the earliest.” The customer wanted a price, a timing or an appointment. They now know that you exist, which they knew before they wrote.

The away message says what happens next

It has one job, and three things to say: you are closed, when a person will reply, and what to do if it can't wait. A dentist in Navrangpura could write this:

“We're shut for the night. Priya replies from 9:30 tomorrow morning. If it's swelling or a broken tooth, call the doctor on this number, any time.”

That is three sentences, and the patient with a swollen jaw now knows more than the patient who got “at the earliest”. Give a clock time, and then keep it. A message that promises 9:30 followed by a reply at 2 is worse than no message, because now you have broken a promise before the first conversation.

“Shortly” is not a time. Write down the hour a person will reply, then make sure a person does.

Change it when your hours change. Diwali is six days away as this goes up, and plenty of Gujarat will shut for it. If you are closed till Labh Pancham, say so and give the date. “We'll reply tomorrow”, sent every night for a week by an empty office, teaches a customer that your promises are automatic too.

The greeting is for a stranger

The greeting goes to someone writing for the first time, or coming back after a long gap. Assume they know nothing about how you work, and use the line to save a round of back and forth. Ask for the thing you would ask anyway.

“Hi, this is the clinic. Tell us what's troubling you and which day suits you, and we'll give you a time.” Now the first reply carries the facts, and your staff can answer it in one go.

Be careful with numbered menus. “Press 1 for prices, 2 for timings” sounds like a call centre, and in the plain app nothing happens when the customer presses 1, except that a person reads a 1 some hours later. If a human is going to answer anyway, let the customer write in their own words.

A new customer writing at night may receive the greeting and the away message one after the other. Send yourself a message from another phone and read what arrives. Two welcomes in a row look like nobody has checked.

Quick replies come from the chats you already have

Do not sit down and imagine what customers ask. Scroll through your last fifty chats and count. The same few questions will turn up: the price, the timings, the address, whether you deliver to Bopal. Write one reply for each, give it a shortcut you can type after a slash, and stop at six or eight. Nobody remembers the twentieth, and an FAQ page is built from the same list if you ever need one.

The best drafts already exist. Someone in your business answers chats better than everyone else. Find their good replies, copy them out, and tidy them. You will get sentences no template has, because they were typed to a real customer by someone who wanted the sale.

Why quick replies read like quick replies

Customers can spot a pasted message, and it usually comes down to a few habits:

  • The greeting in the middle of a chat. “Dear Sir/Madam, greetings of the day” is strange when you were talking a minute ago. Start with the answer.
  • No gap for a human. Leave a blank to fill in, such as the product they asked about. It forces whoever is sending to read it, and it makes the reply fit the question.
  • No next step. End with what you want them to do: “Shall I book Thursday?” is a better last line than “Please revert”.
  • The wrong language. If a customer writes in Gujarati, an English template feels like a notice from the bank. Keep your top three replies in both, written by someone who speaks it. More in Gujarati or English.
  • The old price. Quick replies keep quoting last year's number after the price goes up. Add them to the list of things you change on that day.

Do this tonight

Wait until you are closed, then send “hi” to your business number from another phone. Read what comes back as a customer would. Does it say when someone will reply? Does it say what to do if it can't wait? Would it be strange coming from a person at your front desk? Then open your quick replies and check every price and address in them. It takes twenty minutes, and these words will go out without you for another few years.

The messages you send first are a separate job, and WhatsApp broadcasts has the rules for those.
