Say what you got wrong in the first sentence.

The customer already knows something went wrong. What they are waiting to find out is whether you know, and an apology that never names the mistake tells them you don't.

Granth Hirapara8 November 20267 minute read

we regret the inconvenience caused. by whom, it does not say.

A cake arrives for a birthday with the wrong name piped on it. A sofa that was promised for Saturday has not left the warehouse by Tuesday. A clinic sends a reminder for an appointment the patient cancelled last week. Each of these businesses will send a message about it, and most of those messages will open with “We regret the inconvenience caused.”

That sentence has no subject and no mistake in it. Something happened, somehow, and it was inconvenient. The customer reads it as a form letter, because it is one, and gets angrier than they were before it arrived.

Name the mistake, in plain words

Start with what you did, said the way the customer would say it. “We piped Riya on the cake instead of Rhea.” “We told you Saturday, and the sofa is still with us.” “We sent you a reminder for an appointment you had already cancelled.”

It feels risky to write it down so bluntly, as if you were handing over evidence. In practice the customer has the evidence already: it is sitting on their dining table. Writing it in their words shows you have understood the problem, and a customer who feels understood is much easier to deal with than one who feels managed.

Use “we”, and leave the staff out of it. “Our delivery partner” and “a new team member” are true more often than not, and they still read as blame passed down the line. The customer bought from you.

Sorry, once, early

Say sorry once, in the first two lines, and mean it. “We're sorry. That was our mistake.” Then stop apologising. A message that says sorry in every paragraph starts to sound like it is asking the customer to comfort you, and the reader has their own evening to get through.

Leave out the phrases that apologise for the customer's feelings instead of your actions. “We're sorry you feel this way” and “sorry if this caused any confusion” both put the problem on the reader's side of the table. So does “any inconvenience”, because the word any suggests there might not have been some.

An apology is about what you did. The moment it starts describing how the customer feels, it has become a complaint about them.

Say what happens next, with a date

The second half of the message is the fix, and it should be as specific as the mistake. “A new cake with the right name will reach you by 6 pm today, at no charge.” “The sofa leaves our warehouse on Thursday morning, and our driver will call you an hour before.” If you are refunding money, give the amount and the day it will show up.

Promise only what you can keep. The same discipline applies here as in a price increase message: the number and the date go near the top, where the reader is looking for them. A second apology for a broken fix is far harder to write than the first.

If you do not know yet, say when you will know. “I'll have a delivery date for you by noon tomorrow” is better than a date you are guessing at.

Explain only as much as helps

A short reason can help, if it tells the customer the problem will not repeat. “The name was copied from an older order with the same phone number. We now read it back to you before baking.” That is one sentence of cause and one of change.

A long reason does the opposite. Three lines about the supplier, the festival rush and the software update read as an excuse, however true they are. The customer does not need to understand your operations. They need to know whether to trust you next time.

Things to leave out

  • “Your satisfaction is our top priority.” It was not, on the day in question, and the customer knows it.
  • A discount code in place of the fix. Offer something extra if you like, but after the problem is solved. Ten per cent off the next order does not put the right name on this cake.
  • Legal or ticket language. “Your concern has been noted and escalated to the relevant team” is the sound of a message being filed.
  • Exclamation marks. They make an apology sound cheerful, which is the one thing it should not be. Copy that sounds nervous has more on why.

Sign it with a name

End with a person, and a way to reach them directly. “If anything else is wrong, message me on this number. Meera, Store Manager.” An apology from “Team” followed by your brand name is an apology from nobody in particular, and the customer has no one to hold to the date you just promised.

Then send it on the channel the customer used to complain. If they wrote to you on WhatsApp, reply on WhatsApp, not by email two days later.

Write yours before you need it

Take the last complaint your business received and draft the reply you wish you had sent, in five lines: the mistake in the customer's words, sorry once, the fix with a date, one sentence of cause if it helps, and a name. Read it aloud. If any line could be sent to any customer about any problem, rewrite that line until it could only be about this one. Keep the result as a template for the shape, and write the words fresh each time. If you would like a second pair of eyes on the draft before it goes, send it to us.
