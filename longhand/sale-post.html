A sale post should say what is cheaper, by how much, and until when.

Most sale posts are a red square, a percentage and the word hurry. The customer learns that something, somewhere, is discounted, and has to ask to find out whether it matters to them.

Granth Hirapara19 November 20266 minute read

limited period offer, now in its third month.

A shoe shop in Maninagar runs an end of season sale. The Instagram post is a red square with “FLAT 50% OFF*” in yellow, “Hurry, limited period offer” underneath, and “*T&C apply” in small type at the bottom. The comments fill up: “Which products?” “Till when?” “Kids' range also?” “Price?” The shop replies “DM” to each one. A few people do. Most move on to the next post.

The post did half its job. It told people there was a sale, and left them to do the rest of the work themselves.

The four things a sale post has to say

  • What is on sale. “All winter jackets”, not “selected items”. If it really is a selection, say which.
  • What it costs now. A price beats a percentage. “Was ₹2,400, now ₹1,499” is something a person can decide on. “Up to 50% off” asks them to guess, and they will assume the thing they wanted is at the bottom of the range.
  • When it ends. A date, and a time if it matters. “Till Sunday 30 November, 9 pm.”
  • How to buy. The shop's address and hours, the link, or “reply to this message with your size”.

If a customer has to comment to find out any of these, the post has made them do your job. Some will. The ones who were only half interested will not, and the half interested are most of the people a sale is for.

Why “hurry, limited period” stopped working

Urgency works when it is true and specific. “Hurry” with no date attached is a mood, and customers learnt a long time ago that the limited period usually gets extended. This is opinion, but it is easy to check against your own feed: the vaguer the deadline, the less anyone believes it. A date is believed because it can be checked.

A deadline only creates urgency if you would be embarrassed to extend it.

If you do extend it, say so, with the new date. Quietly posting “last day” for a week teaches your followers that your last day means nothing, and that lesson outlasts the sale.

Say why there is a sale

A reason makes a discount believable. Without one, a big number makes some people wonder what is wrong with the product. With one, the number makes sense.

“We are clearing last winter's jackets before the new ones arrive in December” tells the customer the jackets are fine, just last year's. “Afternoons are quiet, so lunch is ₹100 off between 2 and 5 on weekdays” tells a cafe customer in Satellite exactly why the offer exists and exactly when to come. The honest reason is usually the better line, because nobody else can write it.

End of season, old stock, a slow month, an anniversary, a new branch: any real one will do. “Mega sale” is not a reason.

The fine print belongs in the post

“*T&C apply” is the asterisk customers trust least. If there are conditions, write them in one plain line: a minimum bill, not on new arrivals, one per customer, not with other offers. A condition the customer reads in the post is part of the deal. A condition they discover at the counter feels like a trick, and they will remember the trick longer than the discount.

The same goes for the volume. Capital letters, three exclamation marks and the word MEGA read as anxious, and anxious reads as desperate. Copy that sounds nervous covers why. A sale is the moment you most want to sound sure of the price.

Where it goes, and how often

  • On Instagram, put the price and the date on the image. Many people will never open the caption. The caption then carries the details and the conditions, and how long a caption should be applies as usual.
  • On WhatsApp, the offer goes in the first line. That is the preview, and the preview decides whether the rest is read. The broadcast piece has the rest. Send it once when the sale starts and once the day before it ends.
  • At the shop, a sign that matches the post. The same price, the same date. A customer who walks in from Instagram should not find a different offer at the door.

One more opinion. A business that runs a sale every month has trained its customers to wait for the next one. Fewer sales, each with a real reason and a real end, are easier to write and easier to believe.

Write yours this way

Before anyone opens a design app, write five lines: what is on sale, the price before and after, the date it ends, how to buy, and why. That is the post. The design only has to make those five lines easy to read at a glance. If you would rather someone wrote the sale posts, and the rest of the feed, that is what we do.
