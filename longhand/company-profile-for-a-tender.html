Your company profile is read by someone with a checklist.

Tenders, vendor registrations and procurement forms all ask for a company profile, and most companies send the brochure. Here is how to write the document a purchase committee actually scores.

Granth Hirapara30 October 20266 minute read

no tender has ever been won on the strength of a vision statement.

Somewhere in every tender document, after the earnest money deposit and before the technical specifications, there is a line asking for a company profile. Most companies attach whatever they have. Usually that is the brochure, sometimes the About page pasted into Word, occasionally a forty-page deck made for a trade fair years ago.

The reader is different from anyone those were written for. A brochure is picked up by someone curious. A profile in a tender file is opened by a procurement officer, or a committee of three, with a list of things to tick before they can shortlist you and a pile of other files to get through before lunch.

Write for the checklist

Read the tender before you write a word. Most of them say, somewhere, what the committee will check: years in business, turnover for the last three years, similar work done, certifications, the people who will do the job, where you are registered. That list is your outline. Use the same headings, in the same order, in the same words the tender uses. If it asks for “experience of similar works”, do not call the section “Our legacy”. The officer is looking for a phrase, and should find it in ten seconds.

Then answer every item, including the ones where your answer is thin. A missing section reads as a missing fact. A short, honest one reads as a small company, which is often allowed.

The first page says what you are

One paragraph, in the plainest words you have: what you make or do, where, since when, and how many people. “We make structural steel for industrial sheds and warehouses. Private limited company, founded 2009, factory in Changodar, 85 people on the payroll.” A reader who stops there already knows if you belong in the file.

Under it, a short table of the facts every form asks for: legal name, registration, GST number, registered address, and one contact person with a phone number that gets answered. Officers copy these into their own sheets. Make them easy to copy.

Take out the mission statement

“To be a globally recognised leader in quality and customer satisfaction.” Nearly every profile has a line like this, and no committee has ever scored it. Nor the founder's message, the photograph of the ribbon cutting, or the page of values with an icon beside each one. They sit at the front, so they push the facts to page four, which is where a tired reader stops looking.

Keep the founder only if the founder matters to the work. An engineer who signs off every drawing belongs in the team section, with qualifications, next to everyone else.

Similar work, written so it can be checked

This is the section that wins or loses the file. For each project: the client, what you did, the size of the job in rupees or quantity, when it finished, and a contact who will confirm it. One project written like that does more than a page of client logos. If a client won't let you use their name, describe them by sector, city and size, the way our piece on writing a case study without naming the client sets out.

A committee can't score adjectives. It can score a project, a year, a value and a phone number that answers.

Put the completion letters and work orders in the annexure and refer to them by number next to each project. The officer who wants proof should not have to hunt for it.

Numbers, in the format they asked for

If the tender asks for turnover in lakhs for three financial years, give it in lakhs, for those three years, in a table. Not crores, not a rounded “over ₹10 crore”, and not a bar chart. Committees compare files side by side, and a number in the wrong unit is one they have to convert. Some will. Some will mark it unclear and move on. The same goes for capacity, headcount and machinery: use their unit, and their order.

The people who will do the work

Many tenders score the team. Name the people who will actually be on this job, with qualifications and experience written as facts. “Site engineer: B.E. Civil, 11 years, three warehouse projects over 50,000 sq ft.” Two lines each is enough. Leave out “dynamic” and “result-oriented”; the committee has read both in every file that morning.

One profile, three lengths

Registration portals, tender files and the PDF a purchase manager asks for on email all want different lengths. Keep one master version, about four pages, and cut it down each time instead of starting again. A one-page summary for registration forms. The full version for tenders. The full version plus annexures where the tender asks for proof. The brochure still has its uses, for the reader who is curious, and our piece on brochures covers that one. It is a different document for a different reader.

Do this before the next tender

Open the last tender you applied for and list every item the committee was asked to check. Then open the profile you sent and, next to each item, write the page number where the answer was. Any item without a page number is a gap. Any page that answered nothing on the list is a page to cut. Do it once, properly, and you have the master version for the next ten files.
