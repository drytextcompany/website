An invitation has to beat the other plans for that evening.

The launch, the seminar, the webinar: the invitation goes out, the replies say “sure”, and half the chairs stay empty. Here is how to write one people put in their calendar and then actually attend.

Granth Hirapara31 October 20266 minute read

“cordially invited” has never moved anyone off a sofa.

Most invitations are written by the person hosting, about the thing they are hosting. The grand launch of our new showroom. A seminar on GST updates. An exclusive webinar with industry experts. Every line is about the event, and none of it is about the person reading it, who is deciding between your Thursday evening and their own.

Saying yes costs nothing. Turning up costs an evening, a drive across town and parking near the venue. The invitation has to make the second decision easy, and most of them only ask for the first.

Lead with what they leave with

Before you write anything, finish this sentence: after this event, the person who came will know or have something they didn't before. If you can't finish it, the event needs work before the invitation does.

Then put that sentence first. “Forty minutes on what the new GST return changes for a trading business, with time for your own questions” tells a CA's client why to come. “Seminar on GST updates” tells them the topic, which they can read about on their phone at home.

For a launch, what they leave with is often simple: they get to see it, touch it or taste it before anyone else. Say that plainly. “The new range is on the floor from Friday. Come on Thursday evening and you see it first, with the people who designed it.”

The facts, where the eye looks for them

Date, day, time, place. People check the day against their week, so give the day of the week next to the date. Give a start time and an end time; an evening with no end time sounds like an evening that won't end. Give the address the way an auto driver would find it, with a landmark, and a map link if the invitation is on a phone. Say whether there is parking.

Then the things people hesitate over and never ask: is it free, is food served, can I bring someone, will anyone try to sell me something. Answer each in a line. The unanswered question is usually the reason for the empty chair.

Say what happens in the room

“Networking and refreshments” means nothing. A short running order means a great deal. “6:30, tea. 7:00, a 25-minute talk. 7:30, questions. 8:00, done.” Now the reader can picture the evening, see where it ends, and plan the drive home.

If nothing is being sold, say so in writing, early. When we wrote the outreach for SELEQT's Investment Awareness Programme, a free session for doctors at their own hospital, that was the rule behind every mail: no product names, no advice, nothing sold in the room. Doctors are written to constantly, and an invitation that smells like a pitch is deleted before the date is read.

Saying yes costs nothing. Turning up costs an evening. Write for the second decision.

One line on who else is coming

People come to rooms where people like them will be. “For owners of manufacturing units in Sanand and Changodar” is an invitation. “Open to all” is a notice. Narrowing the audience on paper usually fills more seats, because the right reader recognises themselves.

If there is a speaker, one line on why they are worth the evening, written as a fact: what they do and for how long. Leave out “renowned” and “thought leader”. If the speaker were renowned, the reader would already know.

Webinars need a narrower promise

An online event feels cheaper to attend, so people register more and turn up less. The link sits in their inbox next to forty others. A webinar invitation needs a narrower promise than a seminar: one question answered, in a stated number of minutes. Say if it will be recorded, because the honest reader who can't make it will still register for the recording.

The reminders are part of the invitation

A single invitation sent two weeks out is forgotten by the day. Plan three messages: the invitation, a reminder the day before with the address or the link, and a short one on the day. The reminder is not a copy of the invitation. It is the two lines a person needs on the morning they are deciding whether to go: the time, the place, and the one thing they will leave with.

On WhatsApp, keep each of them short enough to read without opening the chat. Our piece on WhatsApp broadcasts covers why. On email, the subject line carries the day and the reason, in that order.

Do this before the next one goes out

Take the draft invitation and underline every sentence about the reader rather than about you. If the underlined part is shorter than the rest, rewrite it. Check it answers: when, where, how long, is it free, is there food, will anyone sell me anything, and who else will be there. Write the running order down. Then write the reminder now, while you still remember why anyone should come.
