How long an Instagram caption should be.

Short, say the people who have never had to explain a product in one line. Long, say the people who once had a post saved a few hundred times. Both are answering the wrong question.

Granth Hirapara7 October 20266 minute read

nobody taps “more” out of politeness.

Every content calendar has a column for the caption, and every team that fills one has had the argument about length. The honest answer will not fit in a spreadsheet cell: as long as the post needs, and not a line longer. That sounds like a dodge, so here is how to work it out, one post at a time.

The first line is the only one everyone reads

Instagram shows a line or two of the caption, then folds the rest behind a small “more”. Most people never tap it. So whatever its length, every caption is really two captions: the line under the picture, which everyone sees, and the rest, which only the interested read.

Write the first line as if it were the whole caption. Someone who reads only that and scrolls on should still know what the post was about. “Exciting news for all our valued customers” fails. “Our Satellite clinic now opens at 8 a.m. on Sundays” passes, and anyone who cares will tap for the rest. It is the same job as a homepage headline, with less room and a lot more competition.

Decide what the post is for, and the length follows

Most captions are one of four things, and each has a natural size.

  • A label. The picture does the work: a new flavour, a finished interior, the team at the Navratri dinner. One line, maybe two. Anything more is the caption explaining a joke the photo already told.
  • A nudge. An offer, an event, a deadline. Three or four short lines: what, when, where, and how to get it. Put the date in the first line, because the date is the news.
  • A note. A small story or an opinion, from the founder or the shop floor. A short paragraph, perhaps two. This is where a brand gets to sound like a person.
  • A lesson. Something useful: how to store saffron, what to ask your CA before March. These can run long, and people save them, as long as every line teaches something.

If you cannot say which of the four a post is, the caption will not know either. That is usually when it turns into a paragraph about quality and eleven hashtags.

Long is fine. Padded is the problem

A long caption that teaches something gets read to the end by the people it was meant for. A long caption that describes the picture, thanks everyone for their support and wishes them a happy Monday gets read by nobody, including the person who approved it.

Cut every line your reader would skip. Whatever is left is the right length.

The test takes a minute. Read the caption aloud to someone who was not in the meeting where it was written. Watch them. The line where their attention drifts is the line that goes, and so is anything after it that only repeats what came before.

Write for one thumb

A caption is read on a phone, often in the gap between two other things. That changes the shape of the long ones.

  • Short paragraphs, with space between them. A solid block of text on a phone looks like terms and conditions, and gets the same attention.
  • One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph needs a second idea, it needs a second paragraph.
  • Facts over adjectives. “Open till 11 on Fridays” does more than “extended hours for your convenience”, and it is shorter.
  • Emoji as punctuation, if your brand uses them at all. One that marks a list is useful. One after every phrase is noise.

One ask, at the end

If the post wants something, a call, a visit, a message, a save, say so once, at the end, in plain words. “Message us MENU and we'll send this week's” is an instruction. “Check the link in bio, tag a friend, share with someone who needs this and turn on notifications” is four instructions, and most readers follow none of them.

A label does not need an ask at all. Not every post has to sell, and the ones that try to usually make the ones that should sell easier to ignore.

Hashtags are filing, not writing

They help the post get found. They do not need to be read. Put the few that actually describe the post at the very end, after the ask, where they cannot interrupt it. Hashtags scattered through a sentence read like someone coughing through it.

Sound the same at every length

The one-line label and the four-paragraph lesson should sound like the same company. That is harder than getting the length right, and it is why we write one page on how a brand sounds before we write its first post. What brand voice is explains what goes on that page, and why it saves arguments later.

So: one line when the picture speaks, a few lines when there is a date, a paragraph when there is a story, and as long as it takes when there is something to teach. Then read it once more and cut the line nobody would miss. There is almost always one.
