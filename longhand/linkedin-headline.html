Your LinkedIn headline is read more often than anything you post.

It sits under your name on every comment, every invitation and every search result. Most founders let LinkedIn fill it in with a job title. Here is what to write instead.

Granth Hirapara5 November 20266 minute read

founder at the company you founded. noted.

The headline is the line under your name. LinkedIn shows it nearly everywhere your name appears: on your posts, beside each comment you leave, in search results and in the invitations you send. For most people who come across you, those few words are all they will ever know.

If you never wrote one, LinkedIn wrote it for you from your current position. So it says “Founder at” followed by whatever is printed on the GST certificate. It is accurate, and it tells a stranger nothing they need. They do not know what the company makes, or whether it sells to people like them.

Write it for the person reading your comment

Picture where the headline is mostly read. Someone is scrolling through the comments on a post about a GST change, and they see your photo, your name and one line. The line has to answer the question they are silently asking: who is this, and is it worth a click?

So write it for a stranger in your market, not for the people who already know you. Your existing clients do not need the headline. The purchase head at a pharma company in Vadodara who has never heard of you does.

What a good one says

Most good headlines carry two things: what you do, in plain words, and who it is for. A third is optional, and it is one fact that makes you specific, such as a town, a number of years or a kind of customer. A few that would work:

  • “Tiles for hotel bathrooms, made in Morbi. Founder.”
  • “CA for D2C brands that have just crossed ₹5 crore. Ahmedabad.”
  • “Export packaging for namkeen and spice makers. Twenty years in Rajkot.”

None of them is clever, and none needs to be. Each one lets the right reader recognise themselves and the wrong reader move on, which saves both of you a sales call.

Use the words your buyer would type into the search bar, not the name of your department. A buyer searches for “export packaging”. Nobody has ever searched for “end-to-end value chain partner”.

The pipe-separated list

The commonest founder headline is a stack of nouns divided by vertical bars: Founder | CEO | Speaker | Mentor | Angel Investor | Proud Father. Each word is true. Together they say you could not decide. A reader stops after the first two, which every other founder has as well.

If the list is long because you genuinely do several things, choose the one this profile is for. A founder who also mentors at an incubator should lead with the work that pays the salaries, and leave the rest to the About section, where the reader who wants more will look.

A title says where you sit. A headline should say what you do for the person reading it.

Put the useful words first

LinkedIn allows 220 characters, but the headline is cut short in most of the places it appears. Under a comment on a phone, you may get a line. So put the words that matter at the start, and treat the rest as a bonus for whoever opens the profile.

That is why “Founder” usually goes last. “Export packaging for namkeen makers. Founder.” still works when it is cut after four words. “Founder & Managing Director at” does not, because the cut arrives before anything useful does. The same rule decides a homepage headline: the reader gives you one line, so the first line has to be the answer.

What to leave out

  • Adjectives about yourself. Visionary, dynamic, result-oriented. Readers discount every word you use to praise yourself, and these get discounted first.
  • The company tagline. A line written for a hoarding rarely sits well under a person's name.
  • Hashtags. They take the space your one useful line needed, and nobody follows them from a headline.
  • A former employer, unless it is the reason a stranger should listen to you. If it is, keep it short.

It has to match the posts

The headline makes a promise, and your posts either keep it or quietly break it. If it says you make packaging for food brands and the last six posts are about cricket and sunrise quotes, the stranger who clicks through feels misled. If you rarely post, the headline is doing almost all the work.

If someone writes your posts for you, agree the headline before the first post, because it sets the subject of everything after it. Our piece on what a LinkedIn ghostwriter does covers the rest of that job, and company page or founder profile covers which account should say what.

Do this in ten minutes

Open LinkedIn on your phone, not a laptop. Find a comment you left recently and read what shows under your name. Cover the name and photo with your thumb, and ask whether a stranger could say what you do and who it is for. If not, write ten versions, each starting with what you do. Cross out any a competitor could also use. Read the survivors aloud, keep the one you could say to a stranger at a wedding without embarrassment, and paste it in. Then change it again the day the business changes.
