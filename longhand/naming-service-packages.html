Silver, Gold and Platinum tell a customer which one costs more, and nothing else.

Most package tables are named after metals or adjectives, so the customer has to read the small print to find the difference. Here is how to name them so the name does that work.

Granth Hirapara7 November 20266 minute read

platinum, for when gold wasn't shiny enough.

A gym in Satellite, a CA firm, a salon, a digital agency: open their price page and there is a good chance you will find three columns called Basic, Standard and Premium. Under each one sits a list of ticks, and the customer is left to work out which ticks matter.

The customer looking at three packages has one question: which of these is for me? A metal name answers a different question, which is which one costs more. The price under it already answers that, so the name has been spent on information the reader had anyway.

Name it after the person it is for

The quickest fix is to name each package after the customer who should buy it. A CA firm offering tax filing could call its three packages “Salaried”, “Freelancer” and “Business owner”. Nobody needs the feature list to choose. They read the three words, find themselves, and look at the price.

A wedding photographer in Ahmedabad could do the same with the occasion: “Ceremony only”, “Ceremony and reception”, “All three days”. The family planning the wedding knows which one they are, and the photographer stops getting calls that begin with “what is the difference between Gold and Platinum?”

If the customer has to read the features to understand the name, the name has done nothing.

Or name it after what is in it

Some businesses sell the same thing to everyone in different amounts. A caterer, a tuition class, a cleaning service. Here the honest name is the amount, or the arrangement, said plainly: “Up to 50 guests”, “Weekend batch”, “One deep clean a month”. These names will never win an award, and they will never need explaining either.

Our own price list works this way. The rows on our website content page are called “A short page” and “A home or landing page”, with the examples beside them. Nobody has to translate them before reading the number.

Say the difference in one line

Under each name, write one sentence that says what changes from the package before it. “Everything in Freelancer, plus GST returns every quarter.” That sentence does more than a grid of fourteen rows, where the reader has to compare ticks across columns like a question paper and usually gives up halfway down.

Keep the grid if you need it for the careful reader, but put it below the one line. Most people will decide on the name and the sentence, and only check the grid to confirm what they have already chosen.

Three is usually plenty

Each extra package is one more comparison the customer has to make before they can say yes. Two packages make a choice. Three give a range. Five start to feel like a test, and a customer who cannot tell them apart tends to fall back on the price and pick the one that feels safest to be seen buying.

If you genuinely sell five different things, they are probably five services, and each deserves its own short description instead of a column in the same table.

Names to leave out

  • Metals and gems. Silver, Gold, Platinum, Diamond. They rank the packages and describe none of them.
  • Basic. It makes the cheapest option sound like a compromise, and the customer who buys it feels slightly told off. “Starter” is kinder, but only if the buyer is actually starting.
  • Made-up words. “Pro Max”, “Growth+” or a name built from your brand's initials. Customers have to learn a vocabulary before they can buy, and most will not bother.
  • Internal codes. Plan A, Plan B, Package 2. These are the names your accounts team uses, and they should stay in the accounts team.

The price goes next to the name

A good name and a hidden price still leave the customer guessing. If you can show the number, show it beside the name, in the same size type. If you cannot, give a range or a starting point. Should your website show prices? covers when to show the number and how to write it when you do.

And give the button under each package a name too. “Book the ceremony package” tells the customer what happens next. “Choose” repeated three times does not. The small words on a website do more selling than most owners give them credit for.

Do this with your price page this week

Open your packages on a phone and cover everything except the names with your thumb. Show them to someone outside the business and ask which one they would buy, and why. If they cannot answer from the names alone, rename them. Write each one as the answer to “who is this for?” or “what do I get?”, add one line that says what changes from the package before, and read all three aloud.
