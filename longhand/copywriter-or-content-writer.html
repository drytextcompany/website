Copywriter or content writer: which do you need?

The two titles get used as if they were the same job, which is how a company hires someone to write blog posts and then asks them for a tagline by Friday.

Granth Hirapara5 October 20265 minute read

we answer to both. just tell us which one you meant.

The difference has nothing to do with talent or seniority. It is about what the words are for, and how long they have to keep working.

Copy has to work in one sitting

Copy is writing that needs someone to do something soon: click, call, buy, reply, remember the name. A homepage headline. An ad. A product page. A cold mail. A hoarding. The reader did not come looking for it, so it has seconds, and every word is fighting for the next one to be read.

Copy is short, rewritten many times, and judged by one thing: what people did after reading it.

Content has to earn trust over months

Content is writing someone chooses to read because it is useful or interesting: a guide, a newsletter, a blog post, a LinkedIn post that teaches something, a page like this one. It sells slowly and sideways, by leaving the reader with the impression that you know what you are talking about.

Content is longer, needs research, and is judged over months: by who finds it on Google, who comes back, and the call that opens with “I read your piece on…”

Copy asks for something today. Content is why they say yes next year.

What happens when you hire the wrong one

Give a copy job to a content writer and you get a homepage that reads like a blog: three paragraphs of background before the point. Give a content job to a copywriter and you get a blog post that reads like an ad: confident, short, and with nothing in it anyone could learn from.

Both writers did what they are good at. The brief simply went to the wrong person, usually because the job title in the brief was the wrong one.

Why the titles are muddled

In Indian job listings, “content writer” has become the catch-all: SEO articles, Instagram captions, the About page, product descriptions, and quite often the tagline as well. “Copywriter” still tends to mean advertising. So the title on a CV tells you less than you would hope about what that person is actually good at, which is why the last section of this piece is about samples, not titles.

How to tell which you need

Ask what the piece has to do, and by when.

  • Someone should act after one read. That is copy. Website pages, ads, landing pages, product descriptions, emails that ask for something, brochures, packaging.
  • Someone should trust you more after reading. That is content. Guides, blog posts, newsletters, reports, founder posts on LinkedIn.
  • Both, on one page. More common than it sounds. A service page has to inform like content and close like copy. A case study tells a story and then asks for the meeting.

Do you need two people?

Usually not, and not at the start. Most small businesses need copy first, because the website and the sales material are what a customer sees before anything else, and content only pays off once there is somewhere good for it to send people. Once the site is right, content is how you keep being found.

Plenty of writers can do both. What changes is the brief. For copy, tell them the one action you want. For content, tell them the one question your customers keep asking. The rest of the brief is identical.

Ignore the job title when you hire

Ask for three samples of the kind of writing you need, not the writer's favourite three. A portfolio full of clever ads tells you very little about whether someone can hold a reader through a two-thousand-word guide, and a shelf of good long-form tells you nothing about a seven-word headline.

Then judge the samples the way the reader would. For copy: would you have done the thing? For content: did you learn something you would repeat to a colleague? If you are also working out a budget, what content writing costs covers both kinds, with our own numbers.
