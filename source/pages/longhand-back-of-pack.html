The back of the pack is read with the product already in hand.

The front has to work from three feet away in bad light. The back is read by someone standing in the aisle, deciding, and later at home by someone who has forgotten how much to use. Here is what to write in the space the declarations leave you.

the front gets it picked up. the back gets it bought again.

A pack is the only piece of your copy a customer holds. Everything else is read on a screen, at a distance, with a thumb ready to scroll. The pack gets picked up, turned over and read for a few seconds while a trolley blocks the aisle, and then read again in a kitchen a week later.

Most of that back panel is not yours to write. The declarations your category requires take the room they take: net quantity, the price, the dates, the manufacturer's name and address, the licence number, the customer care line, the barcode. Your printer and whoever signs off compliance decide where that block sits and how small it can legally go. Do not fight it. What is left is usually a panel the size of a visiting card, and that is the writing that does the work.

Start with the question the shelf raises

Stand where your product sits and ask what a stranger would not know from the front. For most packs the answer is one of three questions: what exactly is this, who is it for, and how do I use it. Pick the one your front does not already answer and give the panel to it. A pack that tries to answer all three in forty words answers none.

You will know you have picked wrong if the panel reads like the front again, louder.

The what-it-is line

Category names are written by the industry, not by customers. “Edible oil, cold pressed” is a classification. “Groundnut oil, crushed in a wooden kolhu, filtered twice, nothing added” is a reason to pay more than the bottle beside it. Write the line in the words someone would use to describe the product to their mother.

One or two specifics beat any adjective: the variety, the town, the process, the number of ingredients. Our piece on writing a product description works the same way, with more room. On a pack you get one sentence, so it has to be the specific one.

How to use it, in the order it is used

This is the most skipped panel on Indian packaging and the one customers look for most. Write it as steps, with quantities as numerals. “One teaspoon in a cup of hot water. Stir for ten seconds. Twice a day, after food.” Say how long the pack lasts at that rate. Say what to do after opening, and where to keep it.

If the product can be used badly, say the wrong way too. A face cream that stings when layered over something else, a masala that turns bitter if it goes in early: one line prevents a bad first use, and a bad first use is the end of the customer.

Every question the panel does not answer is a question answered later on WhatsApp, or not at all.

The story panel, and whether it stays

Many packs carry a paragraph about the family, the village, the small kitchen where it all began. Keep it only if it contains a fact a competitor cannot print. “Made in Rajkot since 1994, by the same three families” earns its space. “Crafted with love and the finest ingredients, keeping tradition alive” is four lines of nothing in a place where you had room for the usage.

Two sentences is the ceiling. If the story is genuinely the reason to buy, it belongs on the front, not in the panel nobody promised to read.

Two languages, and which goes where

Decide language panel by panel rather than pack by pack. A pack sold in Rajkot and Junagadh can carry the what-it-is line and the usage in Gujarati and English, and the story in one language only. A pack going to five states carries English and Hindi and loses the story. Our piece on Gujarati or English sets out how to choose, and the same test applies here: write in the language the reader thinks in while deciding.

Translating word for word is where this falls apart. Get the usage instructions written in Gujarati, not translated into it, and read them aloud to someone who cooks.

It is read in a fist, under a tube light

The physical conditions decide half the copy. Dark type on a light ground, never white type over a photograph of the product. Short lines, because a pack is held at an angle. Bigger type than the designer wants, because the person reading is often over fifty and without their glasses. If the artwork has to be zoomed on a laptop before you can read it, it is not readable on a shelf.

Group the panel into two or three small blocks with a bold first word each, so a reader can find the bit they came for. It is the same rule as a menu: people do not read these things, they scan them for the line that concerns them.

Do this before the next print run

Print the back panel at actual size on plain paper, cut it out and hold it. Read it aloud in a kitchen, not at your desk. Mark every line that is about you rather than the product in the hand. Then check it answers what this is, how to use it, how much, how long it keeps and who to call. If the panel passes that and still has room, you have space for the two sentences of story. Most packs do not.
