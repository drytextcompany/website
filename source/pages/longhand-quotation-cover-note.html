The lines above the price decide how the price reads.

A client opens your quotation and looks for the total. Everything they read before they find it, and the first thing they read after, decides whether the number feels fair or steep.

Granth Hirapara13 November 20266 minute read

we are pleased to submit our most competitive rates. said every quote ever sent.

A fabricator in Vatva quotes for a shed at a factory in Sanand. The PDF opens with “Dear Sir, with reference to your enquiry, we are pleased to submit our most competitive quotation for the below mentioned work.” Then a table of fourteen rows, a total of ₹18,40,000, and the terms. Two other fabricators have sent the same thing with different numbers. The client compares the totals, because the totals are the only thing that differs.

The few lines above the table are the only part of a quotation where you get to talk. Most businesses spend them on a greeting. Used properly, they turn a price into an answer.

Say back what they asked for

Open with the job, in the client's words, as you understood it from the call or the site visit. “A 40 by 60 foot shed over the loading bay, roof sheeting included, finished before the monsoon.” One or two sentences.

This does two things. It shows you listened, which the other quotes do not. And it lets the client catch a misunderstanding before they compare numbers, because a quote that is cheaper because it left out the roof is not cheaper.

If the client mentioned something that mattered to them, put it here too. “You said the trucks cannot stop for more than two days.” Nothing in the table will ever say that, and it is often the reason they pick a vendor.

Say what the price covers, in a sentence

The table lists items. The note should say, in plain words, what the client gets for the total. “This covers design, fabrication, erection and the roof, with work done on two weekends so the loading bay stays open.” A client skimming on a phone will read that sentence and skip the fourteen rows.

Then say what it does not cover, just as plainly. “Civil work for the foundations is not included; your contractor will quote for that.” Exclusions hidden in the terms on page three are the usual source of the argument at the final bill. Said up front, they are just information.

A client cannot compare your quote with another one if they cannot tell what yours includes. Tell them, and you are compared on the work.

Give the number one line of reasoning

If your price is higher than they might expect, say why, once, without apologising. “We have quoted for heavier columns than the minimum, because you mentioned adding a crane rail next year, and this saves rebuilding then.” That is a reason the client can check and repeat to whoever signs off.

Do not defend the price at length. Three sentences about quality, experience and commitment read as nervousness. One specific reason reads as a decision you made on their behalf. The same thinking sits behind whether to show prices at all: a number with a reason next to it is easier to accept than a number alone.

If you are offering options, name them so the client can tell them apart without reading the rows. Naming your service packages covers how.

Things to leave out of the note

  • “We are pleased to submit”. Everyone is pleased. The client has never once been reassured by it.
  • “Most competitive rates”. A claim every quote makes is a claim no quote makes.
  • The company history. Your years in business and your list of clients belong in a company profile, if anywhere. The cover note is about this job.
  • “Awaiting your valued order”. It closes the note with nothing for either side to do.

End with the next step and a date

The last lines should say how the client goes ahead and when you will speak next. “If this works, a 30 per cent advance lets us order steel this week. I'll call you on Monday to go through any questions.” Then make the call on Monday.

That last sentence saves you a lot of writing later. A quote that ends with a promised call has its follow-up built in; a quote that ends with “awaiting your response” leaves you with nothing to send but a reminder. Following up on a quote picks up from there.

Sign it with a name and a direct number, not just the company letterhead. Questions about a quote are usually small, and people ask small questions of a person, not of a company.

Write yours now

Take the last quotation you sent and cover the table with your hand. Read only what is left. Could the client tell, from that alone, what you understood the job to be, what the total covers, what it leaves out, and what happens next? If the answer is no, write five lines that say those four things, and put them above the table on the next one. If you would like someone else to write them, that is what we do.
