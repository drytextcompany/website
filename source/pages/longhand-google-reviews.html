Reply to a review for the person who reads it next.

Every reply under a Google review is public, and it stays there for years. The reviewer reads it once. Everyone deciding whether to walk in reads it after them.

Granth Hirapara3 November 20266 minute read

thank you for your valuable feedback. forty times, in a row.

A dental clinic in Navrangpura has forty-one reviews on its Google listing. Forty have the same reply: “Thank you for your valuable feedback.” The forty-first, a one-star review about a two-hour wait, has no reply at all. Someone looking for a dentist at eleven at night reads the lot, and what they learn is that nobody at the clinic reads the reviews.

Most owners treat replies as a courtesy to the reviewer. They are more useful thought of as copy. The review section is the only public conversation most small businesses ever have, and the stranger choosing between you and the clinic down the road is reading every line of it.

Reply to the kind ones like a person

A five-star review deserves more than a thank-you that could sit under anyone's review. Pick one thing the reviewer said and answer it. “Glad the root canal was easier than you were dreading, Mr Shah. See you in two weeks for the crown.” Two lines, one detail, their name.

The detail does two jobs. It tells the reviewer you read what they wrote, and it repeats something specific and good about you for the stranger, in a reply that sounds like a human being typed it.

Write each one fresh. A column of identical replies reads like a machine was set up to answer, the same problem as a WhatsApp away message nobody wrote. And leave the keywords out. “Thank you for choosing the best dental clinic in Ahmedabad” turns the reviewer's compliment into an advert for yourself, and readers notice.

The vague ones

Three stars and no words. Or one word: “Good.” These are easy to skip and worth answering briefly, because a reply shows the listing is looked after. “Thank you, Nisha. If there's anything that would have made it five, we'd like to hear it.” Some people will tell you. The ones who do are handing you the next thing to fix.

The angry ones: wait an hour, then write four lines

Never reply to a bad review in the first hour. The first draft is always a defence, and defences read badly in public. When you have cooled down, write four lines:

  • The specific complaint, in their words. “You waited nearly two hours past your appointment on Saturday.”
  • Sorry, once, if you got it wrong. “That should not have happened, and we're sorry.”
  • What has changed, if anything has. “We now leave a gap after every long procedure so the evening doesn't run late.”
  • A name, and a way to talk privately. “Please message me on the number on this listing. Hetal, Clinic Manager.”

The angry reviewer may never come back. The reply is for the forty people who read their review before they book.

Do not argue, even when the review is unfair. If a fact is wrong, correct it once and calmly: “We have no record of a visit on that date, and we'd like to understand what happened.” Then move the conversation off the page. A reader who sees a business win an argument with a customer remembers the argument, and forgets who won it.

Never put the customer's details in a public reply. No order numbers, no treatment, no “as you know, your payment was pending”. For a clinic or a law firm, that is a breach of the very trust the review was about.

Things to leave out

  • “We regret the inconvenience caused.” It names no mistake and no person, and it reads as a form because it is one.
  • Offers made in public. “We'll give you a free cleaning” under a complaint is an invitation for the next complaint. Make offers in private.
  • Exclamation marks under a complaint. They make you sound pleased about it.
  • The signature “Team”. A team cannot be messaged. A manager can.

The reviews you do not have yet

A handful of bad reviews hurts most when there are few good ones around them. The fix for that is asking more happy customers to write one, at the moment they are happiest, and asking for a testimonial covers how. The paragraph that sits above all of this on your listing is worth the same care, and the Google listing piece goes through it.

Do this tonight

Open your Google listing and sort the reviews by lowest rating. Reply to the three most recent unanswered ones with the four lines. Then pick five recent good reviews and answer each with one detail from what they wrote. Read all eight as a stranger would, on a phone, deciding whether to book. If two of your replies could be swapped without anyone noticing, rewrite one. If you would rather someone else wrote the difficult ones, send them to us.
