A press release is written for the reporter, not the founder.

A reporter on a city desk reads dozens of releases a day and uses a few. The ones that get used are the ones that save her work: the news in the first line, the facts in order, a quote a person might say.

Granth Hirapara17 November 20266 minute read

“proud to announce” is the founder talking to the founder.

A school in Gandhinagar opens a new science block. The trustee asks the office to send something to the papers. The release that goes out begins: “With immense pride and gratitude, the management of the school is delighted to announce a landmark milestone in its glorious history.” The block, the date, and the fact that it is open to students from other schools on Saturdays appear in the fourth paragraph. Nobody prints it. The reporter had stopped reading at “glorious”.

The release did contain news. It was simply buried under the feeling of having news.

Find the news before you write a word

A reporter is asking one question: why would a reader in this city care about this today? Write your answer in one sentence before you start. If the only honest answer is “because we are proud of it”, you have a social post, not a release.

Things a local desk tends to find worth a few paragraphs:

  • Something people can go to, use or buy. A clinic that now opens on Sundays. A library open to the public.
  • Something that changes a place. A new unit in an industrial estate, a road you have paid to fix outside your shop.
  • A first, if it is a real one. The first of its kind in the district, said only if you can stand behind it.
  • A number the reader can picture. Jobs created, seats added, kilometres covered. Real figures only.
  • A person with a story. The student, the worker, the customer, with their permission.

An anniversary, a logo change or a new website are usually not news, however much they mattered internally.

Write the first paragraph as the story

The first paragraph should be usable on its own, as if the reporter printed only that. Who, what, where, when, and why it matters, in two or three sentences. “A new science block at a Gandhinagar school will be open to students from any school in the city on Saturday mornings from 1 December, free of charge.” That sentence can run as it is.

Everything after the first paragraph goes in order of importance, so a desk short of space can cut from the bottom without losing the point. Details next, then background, then the history of the institution, if anywhere.

Write the release so that the reporter could cut it after any paragraph and still have a story.

A quote should sound like a person said it

Every release has a quote from the founder, and most of them read like this: “We are committed to excellence and to serving society.” No person has ever said that out loud, and no reporter will print it. A good quote adds something the facts cannot: a reason, a worry, a plan.

“We had a lab that sat empty every weekend. Children from schools without one were travelling to tuition centres to do experiments. It seemed a waste not to open the door.” That sounds like a trustee, and it explains the decision in a way the first paragraph did not.

Write the quote, then read it aloud. If it would sound strange said across a table, rewrite it until it does not.

The practical parts that decide whether it gets used

  • A plain headline. The news in eight to twelve words. Not a pun, and not the name of your company in capitals.
  • Gujarati and English, if you want both papers. A Gujarati desk is more likely to use a release that arrives in Gujarati, written properly rather than run through a translator. Gujarati or English covers why it matters.
  • One page. Three hundred words is plenty. Background goes in a separate note for anyone who wants it.
  • Two or three photographs, attached, with captions. Named people, left to right. A release with a usable photo has a better chance than one without.
  • A name and a phone number that will be answered. Reporters call to check. If nobody picks up, the story goes to someone who did.
  • A subject line that is the headline. “Press release” tells them nothing. The subject lines piece applies here exactly.

Things to leave out

  • “Proud”, “delighted” and “privileged”. Your feelings are not the news. Every reporter skips them.
  • The list of dignitaries. Name the one who matters to the story. The rest can be in the photo caption.
  • A mission statement. The same advice as for a company profile: say what you do, plainly, in one line at the bottom.
  • Asking when it will be printed. Send it, make yourself available, and call once if you have something new to add.

Try this with your next one

Before you write, finish this sentence: “A reader in this city should care because…”. Then write the first paragraph so it answers that on its own, add one quote you would say out loud, and put a phone number at the bottom. If you would rather someone wrote the release, in Gujarati and English, that is what we do.
