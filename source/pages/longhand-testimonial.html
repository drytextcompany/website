Ask for a testimonial you can actually use.

Most testimonials say the team was very professional and comes highly recommended. They are true, they are kind, and they persuade nobody, because the customer was never asked anything worth answering.

Granth Hirapara9 October 20266 minute read

“great service” is a review of nothing.

Every business has a few customers who would happily say something nice about it. The trouble starts when you ask them to write it down. They are busy, they are not writers, and a week later a message arrives: “Very professional team, great experience, highly recommended.” You thank them, put it on the website, and it sits there beside three others that say the same thing.

The customer did their best. The request was the problem. “Could you write us a testimonial?” asks a busy person to do a writer's job, unpaid, on their phone.

Why most testimonials say nothing

A testimonial works when a stranger reads it and recognises themselves. “Highly recommended” gives them nothing to recognise. It has no before, no problem and no detail, and it could sit under any business in the city. Readers know this, which is why they scroll past the testimonials section the way they scroll past a cookie banner.

The useful ones share a shape. They say what the customer was worried about, what happened, and one specific thing they noticed. “I'd put off the root canal for two years because I was scared of the pain. The doctor explained every step before she did it, and I was back at work the same afternoon.” Nobody writes that when asked for a testimonial. Almost anyone would say it if asked the right questions.

Ask while they still feel it

The best time to ask is just after the customer has felt the result: the day the new shop opens, the week after the wedding, the morning the first clean set of accounts comes back. Ask three months later and you get a polite memory. Ask that week and you get the actual relief, in their own words.

Ask four questions instead

Do not ask for a testimonial at all. Ask these:

  • What were you worried about before you came to us? This gives you the before, which is the part a new customer recognises.
  • Why did you choose us over the others you looked at? Worth knowing even if it never goes on the website.
  • What happened that you didn't expect? This is where the specific detail lives.
  • What would you tell a friend who was thinking about it? Usually this answer is the quote, already in the voice of someone talking to a friend.

Each one can be answered in a sentence or two, and none of them asks the customer to praise you. The praise turns up anyway, with evidence attached.

Nobody can write a good testimonial on demand. Almost anyone can answer a good question.

Make it easy to say yes

In India most of this will happen on WhatsApp, so write the request as a message a person would want to answer. Say why you are asking and how long it will take. Something like: “We're putting a few customers' words on our website. Could I ask you four quick questions? A voice note is fine, or a five-minute call, whichever is easier.”

The voice note matters more than it looks. Plenty of people who would never type four paragraphs will happily talk for two minutes while waiting for their chai. Send the request to one person at a time, by name. Most of the rules in writing a WhatsApp broadcast people don't mute apply here, with one more: this is the message that should never be a broadcast.

If they say no, or do not reply, let it go. A testimonial chased three times reads like one.

Edit lightly, then show them

The answers will be longer than you need, which is good. Pick the two or three sentences that carry the worry, the detail and the result, and trim the rest. Keep their words. Fix only what makes it hard to read. If your edited version contains any of the nine words every Indian brand overuses, you have edited too far, and it now sounds like your brochure.

Then send it back before you publish: “Here's how we'd like to use what you said. Is this all right?” Ask, in the same message, how they would like to be named. A full name with “runs a pharmacy in Maninagar” is worth five quotes signed “R.P., Ahmedabad”, because a reader can picture the first and has to take the second on trust.

Put them where the doubt is

A testimonial is most useful next to the decision it helps with. A quote about fair pricing belongs near the prices. A quote about nerves belongs beside the booking button. One or two can sit on the About page, where people go to decide whether to trust you, and the About page test explains what else that page should be doing.

A separate page called Testimonials, with thirty quotes in a grid, is where they are least read. Nobody goes looking for it.

So this week, pick one customer who was pleased recently, send them the four questions, and offer a voice note. One quote with a worry, a detail and a name will do more for the website than a page of “highly recommended”.
