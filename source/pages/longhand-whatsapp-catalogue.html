Every item in a WhatsApp catalogue needs a name a customer can say back to you.

Most WhatsApp catalogues are forty photos called Product 1, Kurti 14 or Special Cake. The customer likes one, and then has to describe it to you, which is where the order slows down.

A home baker in Bopal sets up a WhatsApp Business catalogue one Sunday. Twenty-two cakes go in, each with a good photo and a price. The names are Chocolate Cake, Chocolate Cake 2, Choco Truffle and Choco Truffle (Big). Most descriptions say “Fresh and delicious” or nothing at all.

On Monday a customer sends a screenshot and asks four things. Is it eggless? How many people does it serve? Can you write a name on top? Do you deliver to Thaltej? The baker types four answers. She will type the same four answers to most of the people who message her this month.

The catalogue had the pictures. It did not have the words, so the chat had to carry them, one customer at a time.

The name is what the customer types back

People order from a catalogue by pointing at something. Some send the item itself. Many take a screenshot, or type what they think it is called. Either way, the name is how the two of you agree on which thing they mean, so it has to tell the item apart from everything near it.

A name that works is the kind of thing, plus the detail that separates it from its neighbours. “Chocolate truffle cake, 1 kg” instead of “Choco Truffle (Big)”. “Cotton kurti, indigo block print” instead of “Kurti 14”. A customer can read either of those aloud on the phone, and you will know which one they mean.

  • If two items could share a name, both names are wrong. Find the difference a customer would notice (size, colour, flavour, fabric) and put it in.
  • Leave out the adjectives. Premium, special, deluxe and classic do not tell anyone which cake it is. They have the same problem as packages called Gold and Platinum, which naming service packages goes into.
  • Codes go after the name, or nowhere. “KT-114” helps your stock register and means nothing to a customer. If you need it, add it at the end.

Two lines underneath: the answers you keep typing

The description does not need to say what the photo already shows. It needs to answer the questions people ask in the chat before they order. You already know what those are. Scroll back through last month's chats and write down every question that came up more than twice. That list is your description.

Two lines is usually enough. The first says what it is, with the fact a photo cannot show: the weight, how many it serves, the fabric, the size range. The second gives the condition that decides whether this person can order at all: how much notice you need, where you deliver, whether it is made to order.

For the baker: “Chocolate truffle, 1 kg, serves 10 to 12. Eggless on request. Order a day ahead; we deliver in Bopal, South Bopal and Shela.” That one entry answers three of Monday's four questions before anyone asks them.

Write the line you would otherwise type into the chat forty times a month.

For the kurti: “Hand block printed cotton, sizes S to XXL. The indigo can bleed in the first wash, so wash it alone once.” The honest line earns more trust than a third adjective would. The longer version of this, for a product page on a website, is in writing a product description. A catalogue is the same work with less room, so keep only the facts that decide an order.

Put the price in, even when it varies

An item with no price gets one message, and it is “price?”. Some people send it. More of them quietly assume it is expensive and close the chat. A catalogue without prices is a list of questions you have asked customers to send you.

If the price really does change with size or order, give a starting point and the reason: “From ₹650 for half a kilo. Price depends on the design.” That is enough for a customer to decide whether to ask the next question.

Arrange it the way people ask

If you group items into collections, group them by what customers come in wanting, rather than by how your kitchen or warehouse sorts them. “Birthday cakes”, “Under ₹500” and “Return gifts” match the first message people send. “Category A” matches nothing.

  • The things you sell most go first. Most people will not scroll to item thirty.
  • Take out what you no longer make. An item still listed after it is gone starts a conversation that ends in “sorry, not available”, which is worse than the customer never seeing it.
  • Point to it from your greeting. The first message a new customer receives can send them to the catalogue. The away message piece covers how to write that greeting so it reads like a person.

Do it in one sitting

Open your last thirty chats and list the questions. Then rewrite the names and the two lines for your ten best sellers, with prices. That will take an evening, and it will cover most of what people order. The rest of the catalogue can follow next week, one shelf at a time.
