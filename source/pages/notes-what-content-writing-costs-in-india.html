Ask ten agencies what a homepage costs and you will get ten versions of “it depends on the scope”. It does depend on the scope. It also depends on nobody wanting to say a number first and lose the negotiation before it starts.

So here is what we see in the Indian market in 2026, what each price actually buys, and the three things that move a quote more than anything else. If you only read one line, read this one.

Per word is how you buy typing. Per project is how you buy thinking.

The three ways people price writing

Per word. The oldest one, and the easiest to compare, which is exactly why it survives. It is also the reason so much Indian web copy reads like it was paid for by the kilo.

Per piece. A post, a script, a page, a deck. You know what you are buying before it is written, and the writer is not rewarded for padding.

Per month. A retainer: an agreed amount of work every month, usually cheaper per piece and more predictable for both sides. It only works once you know roughly how much you publish.

What the money actually buys

Rough ranges, from what we see quoted in India right now, for English copy:

  • Under a rupee a word. Bulk content, usually written fast and often assisted by a tool. It fills a page. It rarely sounds like anyone, and it will not survive a reader who is deciding whether to trust you.
  • One to three rupees a word. A competent freelancer who will do what the brief says. Good value when the brief is already good.
  • Three to ten rupees a word, and up. People with a specialism: finance, health, technology, or a category where getting it wrong is expensive. You are paying for what they already know.
  • Per project, four figures to six. Agencies. The number covers the thinking, the options you get to choose from, and somebody being responsible for the result.

None of those tiers is wrong. A festival post for a local café does not need a specialist. A homepage for a business where one bad sentence costs a client does.

The three things that move a quote

Research. A caption about a new flavour takes twenty minutes. A carousel explaining what a nominee legally is takes a morning of reading and a phone call, because a mistake in it is not embarrassing, it is dangerous. Same 90 words.

Revisions. Any honest quote assumes a number of rounds. Ours assumes you will have opinions, because you should, and we would rather build that into the number than argue about it later.

Responsibility. A freelancer delivers a document. An agency is on the hook when the launch is on Friday and the client's founder wants a different line on Thursday night. That difference is most of the price gap, and nobody writes it on the invoice.

Why we quote per project

Because a word costs nothing. Deciding which word costs everything, and per-word pricing quietly punishes the version that is shorter and better.

Here is a real shape of a small job. A café wants its Instagram taken seriously. Before a single caption, we write one page on how the brand should sound: who it talks to, its tone, what it never says. Then the first month of posts, each one either worth stopping for or not written. Priced per piece, so if you post less in December you pay less in December.

And if it grows, we say so before it grows, not after.

Five questions to ask anyone before you pay

  1. What is included, in pieces, not in words? “Four posts and one script” is a scope. “2,000 words” is a weight.
  2. How many rounds of changes, and how fast do they come back? A day is reasonable. A week means you are in a queue.
  3. Who actually writes it? Ask for two pieces they wrote for someone in a category like yours.
  4. Is any of it written by a tool? Not a trick question. Just get the answer before, not after.
  5. What happens if I hate the first draft? The answer tells you everything about how the next three months will go.

The short version

Cheap writing is not expensive because of what you pay. It is expensive because you pay again, in the rewrite, and in the months the page spent saying nothing.
