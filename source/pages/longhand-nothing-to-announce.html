Most weeks have no news. Post anyway.

Founders wait for something to announce, and most weeks nothing arrives. The weeks without news are full of things your clients would like to read. You are standing too close to see them.

Granth Hirapara15 November 20266 minute read

no news is not no post. the quiet weeks are most of the year.

A founder in Ahmedabad runs a company that makes packaging for sweet shops. She has a LinkedIn profile, a decent network and a vague guilt about both. Her last post was in August, when they opened a second unit. Since then, nothing has happened that feels worth a post. No award, no big order she is allowed to name, no hire. So she posts nothing, and the profile slowly starts to read like a closed shop.

Announcements are the rarest thing a business produces and the thing people think a post must be. The result is a feed with four loud weeks and forty-eight silent ones. A founder who posts in the silent weeks has most of the room to themselves.

Your ordinary week is the material

Look back over the last seven days for anything that made you pause. These usually count:

  • A question a customer asked. If one person asked it, ten others wondered. Answer it in a post.
  • A mistake, small enough to say out loud. A wrong delivery date, a proof that went out with a typo. What you changed afterwards is the post.
  • A decision you made and the reason. Why you refused a job. Why you moved the cut-off time. Why you stopped doing something everyone in your trade does.
  • Something you disagree with. A habit in your industry that you think is wrong. State it plainly, in one paragraph.
  • A thing you noticed on the floor, at the counter or on a call. Specific and small beats large and general.

None of these needs permission from a client or a launch date. They need you to have been paying attention, which you were.

Write it in under ten lines

A post with no news cannot lean on the news to hold attention, so it has to be short and clear. A shape that works:

The first line says the thing. Not a build-up, not “I have been thinking lately”. “A customer asked me last week why our boxes cost more than the ones in the market.” The reader knows within a second whether this is for them.

The next three to six lines give the specifics: what you said, what is true, what you would tell the next person who asks. Use numbers if you have real ones, and leave them out if you do not.

The last line is where you stop. Do not ask the network to “share their thoughts in the comments”. If you have a real question, ask it. If you do not, end on the point.

A post that says one small true thing, plainly, does more for a founder's name than a loud announcement made once a year.

Leave out what makes a quiet post sound loud

  • The humble opener. “Excited to share” and “humbled to announce” promise news the post then fails to deliver.
  • The manufactured lesson. A post about a chai stall does not have to end in a business principle. If the lesson is not obvious, leave it out.
  • The stack of hashtags. Two at most, and only if they are words a person in your trade would search.
  • The borrowed story. The viral tale of the CEO and the cleaner has been told by a thousand founders. Tell your own, or none.
  • Praise for yourself disguised as gratitude. “Grateful to my amazing team” followed by a list of your results is still a list of your results.

How often, and what to do about the silence

This part is opinion. Once a week is enough for a founder with a business to run, and twice is plenty. Pick a day and keep it, because a rhythm is easier to hold than a mood. Write the post in ten minutes on a phone, read it once aloud, and send it.

Some posts will get a handful of reactions. That is normal, and it is not the measure. The measure is the person who reads three of your posts over two months and then mentions one when you finally speak. Your headline tells a stranger who you are; the posts are what make them stay. And where the posts belong, on your profile or the company page, is covered in company page or founder profile.

If you cannot find the ten minutes, that is a fair reason to hand it over. What a LinkedIn ghostwriter does, and can't is a plain account of how that works: you supply the week, we supply the sentences.

Open your phone's notes and write down three things from this week that made you pause: a question, a mistake, a decision. Pick the one you would tell a friend over chai.
