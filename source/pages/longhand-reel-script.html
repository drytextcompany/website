The thumb decides in about the time it takes to read this sentence. Here is how to write a script for that thumb, from the first line to the last.

A Reel script is short, usually fifteen to thirty seconds, and most are written in the wrong order. The owner starts with the logo, then the name, then a greeting, then the offer. The viewer, who never asked to see any of it, has gone by the second word.

The person holding the phone did not search for you. They were watching a cousin's sangeet and a cooking clip, and yours arrived between them. So write for someone who owes you nothing and whose thumb is already moving.

The first two seconds carry the whole Reel

The opening has to make one particular person stop. The simplest way is to say, out loud and on screen, the thing that person is already thinking. A physiotherapist in Bodakdev could open with: “If your back hurts when you get out of the car, watch this.” A sweet shop could open on a hand breaking a fresh kaju katli, with no words at all.

Both work because the viewer recognises something at once. The openings that fail share a few habits: a logo animation, “Hello everyone, welcome back”, a slow pan across the shop front, or a question so broad it applies to nobody, such as “Want to grow your business?”

Write the first line last. You only know what it should promise once you know what the Reel delivers.

Put that first line on screen as text as well as in speech. Plenty of people scroll with the sound off, on a bus or in a waiting room, and the words on screen are all they get.

Promise one thing, then keep it

The opening makes a promise, and the middle keeps that one and no other. If the physiotherapist said “watch this”, the next fifteen seconds show the stretch, slowly, from an angle the viewer can copy. Nothing about the new equipment, the doctor's degrees or the monsoon offer. Those are other Reels.

One idea per Reel is the rule broken most often, because a shoot feels expensive and the owner wants to use it for everything. A Reel that tries to say five things starts to feel like an advert, and viewers have had years of practice at skipping adverts.

Write it in two columns

A Reel runs two tracks at once: what is seen and what is heard. Write them side by side, one line per shot. A sweet shop's Reel for the wedding season might look like this on paper:

  • Shot 1. Hands breaking a kaju katli, close up. On screen: “Made at six this morning.”
  • Shot 2. The karigar rolling the next batch. Voice: “We make it every day, in small batches.”
  • Shot 3. Boxes stacked for a wedding. Voice: “Wedding orders need four days' notice.”
  • Shot 4. The shop front. On screen: “Near Manek Chowk. Order on WhatsApp.”

Writing it like this shows the problems before anyone picks up a phone. If a line of voice has no shot to go with it, you will end up filming the owner talking at the camera. If two shots say the same thing, one of them goes.

Write for the mouth, then cut it in half

Spoken words take longer than you expect. Read the script aloud with a timer running. A thirty second Reel holds far fewer words than a caption, and a first draft nearly always runs long. Cut the greeting, the “basically” and the second example, and read it again.

Write the way the person on camera actually talks. If the owner explains things to customers in Gujarati, a script in formal English will sound read off a card, and viewers hear that immediately. The test from Hinglish in brand copy applies here too: does anybody at your company talk like this, and does your customer?

The last line is the only call to action

End with one instruction the viewer can follow from where they are sitting. “Save this for the next time your back hurts” suits a stretch Reel, because saving takes one tap and nobody has to leave the app. “Order on WhatsApp, the number's in the bio” suits the sweet shop. “Like, share, comment and follow” is four instructions, which works out the same as none.

The caption underneath has a different reader: the person who stayed to the end and wants more. How long an Instagram caption should be covers that part.

Do this before the next shoot

Open your last five Reels and watch only the first two seconds of each, with the sound off. Ask what a stranger would know at that point. If the answer is your logo, rewrite the opening of the next one first. Then write the whole thing on one sheet in two columns, and read it aloud with a timer before anyone films.
