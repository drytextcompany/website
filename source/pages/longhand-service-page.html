A service page should say what happens after they call.

Ten firms in Ahmedabad offer GST filing, and most of their service pages carry the same four bullets. A buyer who cannot tell them apart picks on price, or on whoever answers the phone first.

Granth Hirapara4 November 20265 minute read

comprehensive, end-to-end, one-stop. three words, no information.

Open the websites of three CA firms and click on GST. Each page will say something close to “We offer comprehensive GST services including registration, return filing, reconciliation and advisory.” Every word of that is true, for all three firms. Which is the problem.

The name of a service is a category. GST filing, pest control, interior design, digital marketing: the buyer already knows what the category is, which is why they searched for it. What they are trying to find out is what your version of it is like. Most service pages answer the first question and never get to the second.

Start with who it is for

The first line should let the right buyer recognise themselves. “For shops and small manufacturers who file every month and do not have an accountant on staff.” “For restaurants that cannot close the kitchen for a day of pest control.”

Narrowing feels like turning customers away. In practice the people outside the description were never going to read past the first line, and the people inside it now feel the page was written for them. A firm that serves everyone sounds like it has not noticed anyone in particular.

Say what happens, in order

This is the part almost nobody writes, and it is the part that differs most between two firms selling the same thing. Write the steps as the customer will live them:

  • What they send you, and how. “You send your sales and purchase bills on WhatsApp by the 5th of each month.”
  • What you do with it. “We check them against your bank statement and flag anything missing within two days.”
  • What they get back. “A one-page summary of what was filed and what you owe, before the return goes in.”
  • Who they talk to. “The same person every month, by name.”

Four plain steps tell a buyer more than a paragraph of adjectives, because they can picture the month. The pest control company can do the same: who visits, what they inspect, how long the kitchen is closed, and when they come back.

Every firm offers the service. Only you can describe how you do it, so that is what the page is for.

Answer the three phone questions

Most calls to a service business ask the same three things: how much, how long, and what do I need to do. Put the answers on the page. Price can be a starting figure or the way you price, and should your website show prices argues for showing at least that much. Time can be a range. What the customer has to do is usually the most reassuring line on the page, especially when the answer is “not much”.

If you have more questions than that, they belong in a short list at the bottom, written from the questions people actually ask. The FAQ piece covers how to find them.

One real piece of proof

One specific thing beats a wall of logos. A sample of the summary report you send each month. A short account of one job, with what was wrong and what you did. A named client who has agreed to be named. If they have not agreed, a case study without the client's name still works, as long as the details are real. Whatever you use, it has to be true, because the buyer will ask about it on the call.

Things to cut

  • Comprehensive, end-to-end, one-stop, hassle-free. They describe every firm and therefore none.
  • A paragraph explaining what the service is. The buyer knows what GST is. They came to find someone to file it.
  • “Why choose us” with quality, trust and timely delivery. Every competitor claims the same three, and nobody believes any of them on sight.
  • Eight services on one page. Our own service page puts it this way: one page per thing you sell, because a page that sells five things sells none. A services index with eight tiles is fine, as long as each tile leads to its own page.

Rewrite yours this week

Take the service that brings you the most work and open its page. Write one line on who it is for, four steps in the order the customer lives them, an answer to how much, how long and what they need to do, and one piece of proof. Then put your page next to a competitor's and swap the logos in your head. If nobody would notice, the page still describes the category.
