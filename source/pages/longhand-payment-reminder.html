A payment reminder should make paying the easiest thing to do.

Most unpaid invoices are sitting in an inbox, waiting for someone to find the PDF, check it, and ask accounts to release it. The reminder's job is to do as much of that for them as it can.

Granth Hirapara16 November 20266 minute read

just a gentle reminder. the fourth gentle reminder.

A photographer in Bodakdev shoots a product catalogue for a kitchenware brand. The invoice for ₹85,000 goes out the day the files are delivered, with thirty days to pay. On day forty she sends a WhatsApp: “Hi sir, just a gentle reminder regarding the pending payment, kindly do the needful at the earliest.” Two blue ticks. Nothing. On day fifty-five she sends the same message with “very urgent” added. The client pays on day seventy, and does not call her for the next shoot.

Neither message told the client which invoice, how much, or how to pay. The first was too polite to be acted on. The second was too sharp to be forgotten.

Put the whole invoice in the message

The person you are writing to is often not the person who pays. They have to forward your message to accounts, and accounts will ask what it is for. So the reminder should carry everything accounts needs, in the body of the message:

  • The invoice number and date. “Invoice 0142, dated 3 October.”
  • The amount, in figures. ₹85,000, with GST stated if it applies.
  • What it was for, in five words. “Catalogue shoot, 42 products.”
  • The due date that has passed. Written as a date, like 2 November.
  • How to pay. The UPI ID or bank details, typed out, so nobody has to dig for the last mail.

Attach the PDF again as well. A client who has to search their inbox for the invoice will do it later, and later is the problem.

Write it as a plain fact

“Gentle reminder”, “sorry to bother you” and “whenever convenient” all tell the client the money can wait. They are written to avoid sounding rude, and they succeed: they do not sound like anything at all. A plain sentence is not rude.

“Invoice 0142 for ₹85,000 was due on 2 November and is still open. Could you let me know when it will be released?” That is the whole reminder. It names the thing, says it is late, and asks a question that needs an answer with a date in it.

Ask when the payment will be made, not whether. A date is something you can hold someone to. “Will do” is not.

The same instinct that fills copy with apologies and exclamation marks is at work here. Copy that sounds nervous covers it in more detail; a reminder is simply the place where nerves cost you money.

Send them on a schedule you decided in advance

This part is opinion. Reminders go wrong mostly because they are sent in a mood: too late, because chasing feels awkward, and then too sharply, because the wait has made you angry. Decide the rhythm when you send the invoice, and the mood stops mattering.

  • Three days before it is due. A friendly note with the invoice attached again. Most late payments are forgetfulness, and this catches them.
  • The day after it is due. The plain reminder above, asking for a date.
  • A week after that. Shorter still, and a phone call the same afternoon. People who ignore a message often answer a call.
  • After that, a different person. Write to whoever signed off the work, or the accounts head, copying your contact. Say only what is owed and since when.

Each message should be shorter than the one before. By the third, it can be two lines.

Things to leave out

  • “Kindly do the needful”. It asks the client to work out what the needful is.
  • The story of your cash flow. Your rent and your staff salaries are real, and they are not the client's reason to pay. Said in a reminder, they sound like pressure.
  • Threats you will not carry out. “We will be forced to take further action” from a two-person studio is read as bluster, and it ends the relationship either way.
  • Capital letters and “URGENT”. If everything about the message is loud, the client hears the tone and misses the amount.

Most of the work happens before the invoice

A reminder is easier to write, and less often needed, when the terms were clear from the start. If the cover note on your quotation said what the advance was, when the balance falls due and how to pay, the reminder only has to point back to it. The same is true of chasing a quote: following up after the silence works the same way, with a fact and a date instead of a plea.

Put the due date on the invoice as an actual date, so nobody has to count thirty days from a delivery they have forgotten. Put the payment details on the invoice itself. And send the invoice the day the work is delivered, while the client is still pleased with it.

Write yours now

Open your list of unpaid invoices and pick the oldest. Write one message with the number, the amount, what it was for, the date it was due, how to pay, and a question asking when it will be released. Read it once as the client's accounts person would. If they could pay from that message alone, send it.
